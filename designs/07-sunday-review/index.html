Cover story / The long read

The quiet politics
of staying put.

Across the country, a generation is choosing the town it grew up in over the city it was told to want. What looks like a retreat may be the most practical politics of all.

By Mara Ellison · Photographs by Ivo Marchand · 18 min read

The bus from the station runs twice an hour, and on Sundays it runs when the driver feels like it. There is one café, one pharmacy, and a shop that sells fishing line beside birthday cards. By the standards of the national conversation, this is not a place young people are supposed to stay.

And yet they do. Not all of them, and not always for good, but enough that the school has a waiting list and the pub has started taking bookings. The reasons are not romantic. Rent is lower. Parents are close. The commute is a road, not a train timetable.

What interests me is what happens after the practical reasons run out. People begin to describe the town as a choice, then as a politics. They talk about the library, the bus, the flood plain. They learn the name of the councillor. They stay for a meeting that could have been an email.
